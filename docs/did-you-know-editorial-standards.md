# Full Circle — Did You Know? editorial and research standard

## Purpose
Make Scripture's world intelligible, memorable and historically concrete. Treat Scripture as authoritative Christian Scripture, not mythology. Write original, child-accessible, intellectually substantial discoveries. This is not a collection of sermon summaries or automatic verse observations. Target at least two individually researched discoveries per chapter across all 1,189 chapters, without manufacturing filler to meet a quota.

## Mandatory research lenses (consider all relevant lenses for every chapter)
1. Daily life: speech, dialect, idioms, etiquette, meals, cooking, homes, sanitation, families, hospitality, festivals, weddings, funerals, mourning, rituals and social institutions.
2. Language: explain unfamiliar words and familiar words whose meanings changed. Provide Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek or relevant Latin lemma, transliteration, attested sense in the passage and practical effect on reading. Distinguish historical etymology from contextual sense. Do not infer meaning merely from a root or modern cognate.
3. People: named historical contemporaries, rulers, officials, authors, neighbors and institutions; document overlapping dates and geography; distinguish verified encounters from possible links.
4. Numbers and unusual facts: quantities, measurements, phrase frequency, first/last canonical occurrence, rare words, meaningful patterns. Verify counts against a named translation or original-language corpus, define search rules; distinguish canonical order from chronological priority; no numerology or arbitrary counting.
5. Names and aliases: multiple names, titles, honorifics, name changes, attested meanings and disputed derivations.
6. Work and tools: occupations, trades, apprenticeships, materials, products, clientele, markets, economic status and technology; ancient sayings and professional customs with dates.
7. Sensory world: sounds, smells, tastes, textiles, lighting, architecture and seasonal environment; label reconstructions and avoid unsupported distances or intensities.
8. Misconceptions: distinguish popular imagery and later traditions from the text; e.g., distinguish angelic messengers, cherubim, seraphim and living creatures rather than assigning every characteristic to every being.
9. Miracles: calculate explicitly supplied quantities and plausible scale using labeled assumptions, units and ranges. Discuss scientific implications conditionally, not as an asserted mechanism. Scripture's description of extraordinary daylight in Joshua 10 does not establish that Earth's rotation stopped; if considering an abrupt halt, explain physical implications only as a hypothetical. Never use fabricated NASA missing-day claims.
10. Prayer and worship: garments, fringes, prayer times, tefillin/phylacteries, tallit, incense, sacred objects, ritual practices; distinguish biblical commands, Second Temple evidence and later customs.
11. Clothing and appearance: dress, head coverings, hair, beards, jewelry, status and cultural contrasts among Hebrew, Egyptian, Greek, Babylonian and Roman communities, with period-specific evidence rather than stereotypes.
12. Hidden connections: rare shared original-language words, explicit quotation, literary echoes, symbols and parallel events. Explain the precise relationship, not merely link passages. Example: Hebrew tevah (H8392) denotes Noah's vessel and Moses' basket, not the covenant ark (aron). Rescue is a thematic connection, not an alternative dictionary meaning.
13. Historical evidence: primary records, artifacts, manuscripts, inscriptions, coins, geography and archaeological research. Explain exactly what is attested and what is not.
14. God's character: distinguish explicit divine action, permission, judgment, human and adversarial agency. Interpret Scripture in light of Christ (John 14:9; Hebrews 1:3), James 1:13, Job 1–2, Exodus 34:6–7. Hebrew satan can describe an adversary or accuser; avoid claiming no pre-exilic person knew of the devil or assigning Satan to passages that do not name him. Mark harmonizations as interpretations and preserve explicit wording.

## Quality and verification
- A discovery must add knowledge that improves understanding of its specific passage. Ordinary verse repetition, generic questions, and manufactured word counts remain excluded. A genuinely rare word, first occurrence, meaningful number or surprising textual pattern is welcome when verified and its relevance is explained.
- Treat user-proposed illustrations as research leads, not established facts. For Acts 18:3, investigate skenopoios (tentmaker; broader leather/textile trade debated), not a categorical claim Paul made luxury cloth for the rich. Investigate rabbinic trade-training maxim (Kiddushin 29a) and distinguish later compilation from direct evidence of first-century practice. Research Temple incense using dated evidence, distinguish literary descriptions of fragrance from measured distance.
- Cite specific primary or credible scholarly sources with title, passage, catalog number, date or stable URL where available. Check source before publishing. Name uncertainty and distinguish archaeological attestation, textual facts, Christian interpretation, and hypothetical reconstruction.
- Relevant, licensed imagery only; credit artist/institution and license, distinguish artifact photograph from reconstruction. Lazy-load and compress images.
- Present short intriguing titles and plain language; add original-language script and transliteration only where useful. Avoid repeating the same discovery across entries; use context-defined threads for related passages.
- Keep Bible and Today's Reading consistent. Audit existing entries against this broadened standard; protect valuable factual and lexical discoveries, rewrite shallow but promising topics, remove filler and redundant entries. Do not execute prior removal recommendations blindly when the expanded criteria change the classification.
- Publish verified batches only; track chapter coverage, source verification and deployment independently.
